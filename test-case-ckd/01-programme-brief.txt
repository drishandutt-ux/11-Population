# Early kidney disease programme — joint-working proposal (TEST DATA)

This is an invented proposal written for testing the tool. The integrated care system, the
practices, the people and every figure are made up. The shape of the programme resembles real
NHS collaborative-working projects so that research finds real material to check it against.

## Who is proposing it

A global, research-driven pharmaceutical company (**the sponsor**) and a northern English
integrated care system (**Northmoor and Coast ICS**), working under the industry's
collaborative-working framework: shared aims, shared resources, published outcomes, and the NHS
keeps clinical decisions. The sponsor makes one of the kidney-protective medicines in the class
the programme uses; prescribing follows national guidance and the ICS formulary, not the sponsor.

## The problem

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is common, silent until late, and most common in the poorest areas.
Around one in ten adults has it. Most people at risk (type 2 diabetes, high blood pressure, heart
disease) should have a blood test (eGFR) and a urine test (uACR) every year. In Northmoor and Coast
the blood test usually happens; the urine test often does not; and when the results show CKD, the
diagnosis is frequently not recorded, so the person is never offered the treatment that slows it.

## The programme, in five steps

1. **Find.** A practice-level search flags every registered adult at risk who has had no uACR or
   no eGFR in the last twelve months.
2. **Invite.** The practice invites them for a blood and urine test. Today this is a letter.
3. **Test.** The person attends, gives a blood sample and a urine sample.
4. **Diagnose and record.** A clinical pharmacist reviews the results, records CKD where present,
   and explains what it means.
5. **Treat and keep treating.** People with CKD are offered kidney-protective treatment (a blood
   pressure medicine of the RAS-blocker type and, where indicated, an SGLT2 inhibitor), with a
   review at three and twelve months.

The programme runs for two years in the ICS's twelve most deprived primary care networks first.

## What the ICS and the sponsor want to know before they sign

1. Of the people flagged, how many will be tested, diagnosed, started on treatment and still on it
   a year later?
2. Where is the biggest drop-off, and is it the same in the most deprived wards as elsewhere?
3. Which changes to the programme would move the most people, and which barriers no programme can move?
4. What outcome should the joint-working agreement commit to, so it can be checked in twelve months?

## What the programme can change (levers)

- **How people are invited:** letter; text message; a phone call from a pharmacy technician;
  a word from the GP at another appointment.
- **Where they are tested:** the practice; the community pharmacy on the high street; a mobile
  clinic in the ward; a urine-sample kit posted home.
- **When:** evening and Saturday slots.
- **Who explains the result:** a letter; a call from the pharmacist; a face-to-face appointment.
- **How treatment starts:** a separate GP appointment later, or the pharmacist prescriber starts
  it at the result conversation.
- **Plain-language materials:** a one-page "what a kidney test means" leaflet in the area's
  main languages.
- **Transport help** for the mobile clinic.

## What the programme cannot change quickly

- GP appointment capacity across the ICS.
- The number of clinical pharmacists in each network (one per network for the programme).
- The national guidance on who is tested and who is treated.
- Long-standing distrust of the NHS in some communities, and the cost of prescriptions for people
  under sixty who do not qualify for free ones.
- The medicine's known side effects (more urine, thrush, a small risk of dehydration), which
  some people stop for.
